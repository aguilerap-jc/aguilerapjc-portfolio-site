Two years ago, Verne was building vehicles. A Croatian company with ambitions to manufacture purpose-built robotaxis, with Mobileye providing the autonomy stack. That was the plan: a European vehicle maker paired with an established technology supplier.

By early 2026, Verne had [quietly shelved its Mobileye partnership](https://www.mobileye.com/news/verne-unveils-urban-mobility-service-driven-by-mobileye/), partnered with Pony.ai for the autonomy software, and was operating Chinese-built Arcfox Alpha T5s on the Uber platform in Zagreb. Safety driver present.

Three value chain positions in roughly two years. Whether the shift from building vehicles to operating them is a temporary bridge or a permanent pivot is not yet clear.

On April 8, 2026, [Verne, Pony.ai, and Uber announced](https://investor.uber.com/news-events/news/press-release-details/2026/Verne-Pony-ai-and-Uber-Partner-to-Launch-Europes-First-Commercial-Robotaxi-Service/default.aspx) what they called Europe's first commercial robotaxi service. Permit discussions are reportedly underway in eleven additional EU markets plus the UK.

The question this raises is not whether Verne will succeed. It is why a company that started by building vehicles ended up operating someone else's technology on someone else's platform. And what that tells us about an industry where nobody has figured out which role actually captures value.

---

## Trying On Roles

The technology question has been answered at commercial scale in enough places to shift the conversation. Waymo, Apollo Go, WeRide. The new question is different: now that the technology works, who does what? Who builds the vehicle, who provides the software, who owns the fleet, who operates the depot, who faces the customer, who bears the liability? In traditional automotive, these roles were settled decades ago. OEMs build. Dealers sell. Fleet companies lease. Drivers drive.

Autonomous vehicles erase those lines. And the industry is redrawing them in real time, through deals that are less about commitment than about experimentation. Verne's three pivots in two years are not indecisiveness. They are rational behavior in an environment where the right role is genuinely unknown.

But one company's search is a manageable puzzle. The ambiguity gets harder when a single company tries to occupy all the roles at once.

---

## The Arms Dealer

If Verne represents a company searching for its role, Hyundai represents one that has decided not to choose.

Hyundai's Savannah, Georgia plant builds the Ioniq 5 for Waymo's robotaxi fleet. From the same production line, the same plant also builds the Ioniq 5 for AVride, an independent company running a competing L4 stack. Two rival autonomy systems, one factory floor.

Then there is Motional, Hyundai's own joint venture with Aptiv. After a near-collapse in 2024 that saw the fleet drop from hundreds of vehicles to roughly 30 in Las Vegas, Motional relaunched with a new CEO (Laura Major) and joined Uber in Las Vegas in March 2026, with fully driverless operations targeted for later that year.

And then there is 42dot, Hyundai's internal subsidiary. A $320 million investment in a camera-plus-radar stack (no lidar) intended for personally owned autonomous vehicles under the Genesis brand. A fourth bet, a third architecture, and a fundamentally different business model from ride-hail.

Four autonomous driving bets. Three distinct sensor architectures. One company.

The logic is not complicated. Hyundai profits from the vehicle sale regardless of which autonomy stack wins. The 42dot investment suggests Hyundai is not content being purely the hardware layer. But for now, the arms dealer position is the safest one in an undefined market: sell to everyone, commit to no one, and let the technology companies fight over the software margin.

That kind of multi-position hedging is uncomfortable. But at least Hyundai's contradictions sit inside one company. The real trust problems start when the conflicts span the companies that need to work together.

## The Investor, the Supplier, and the Competitor

NVIDIA's position makes Hyundai's four-way bet look straightforward.

In early 2026, NVIDIA participated in Wayve's $1.5 billion Series D. Wayve is building an end-to-end AI approach to autonomous driving: mapless, camera-first, designed as a licensable stack that OEMs can deploy across their fleets. NVIDIA's investment backs this vision.

Simultaneously, NVIDIA is building its own competing full L4 autonomy stack through NVIDIA DRIVE AV. This stack, including NVIDIA's own compute hardware, is the technology backbone of a memorandum of understanding with Stellantis, Foxconn, and Uber: 5,000 vehicles targeted for 2028 start of production. NVIDIA is not just contributing silicon. It is providing the entire autonomous driving system.

And at CES 2026, NVIDIA open-sourced [Alpamayo](https://www.forbes.com/sites/jonmarkman/2026/01/07/meet-alpamayo-nvidias-new-ai-model-for-autonomous-vehicles/), a driving research model released for non-commercial use. The pitch: become the platform layer that every AV company builds on.

Industry commentary has flagged the conflict of interest directly. NVIDIA is simultaneously an investor in Wayve (backing a licensable third-party stack), a builder of a competing first-party stack (through the Stellantis deal), and a silicon supplier to virtually every other AV company in the industry. If you are a startup deciding whether to build on NVIDIA's platform, you are also deciding how much to trust a company that simultaneously funds your competitor and builds its own alternative to your product.

Hyundai hedges by holding contradictory roles inside a single company. NVIDIA hedges by holding contradictory roles across companies that depend on it. Both strategies are rational. The conflicts of interest would not survive a stable value chain. They work precisely because there is no stable value chain.

The complexity escalates again when you add more parties. One company hedging its own portfolio is chess. Four companies trying to assemble a value chain from scratch, with no one in charge, is improvisation.

## Can Four Strangers Build What One Company Couldn't?

The Stellantis-NVIDIA-Uber-Foxconn MoU represents the extreme case of the industry's structural experiment.

Four parties. Four distinct roles. Stellantis manufactures the vehicles. NVIDIA provides the full L4 autonomy stack. Foxconn handles systems integration. Uber provides fleet operations and the consumer marketplace. Non-binding. 5,000 vehicles. 2028 start of production.

Each component makes sense in isolation. Uber has indicated interest in fleet ownership arrangements, operating through third-party partners, similar to the model already taking shape in Nashville and Dallas. So the ownership question is likely answerable. The harder questions are not. Who bears liability across four parties when something goes wrong? Foxconn's L4 autonomous vehicle integration track record is not yet publicly established. NVIDIA's only confirmed L4 deployment to date is [a partnership with Mercedes](https://www.reuters.com/business/autos-transportation/mercedes-offer-autonomous-driving-tech-us-city-streets-2026-01-05/), still in its early stages.

The traditional automotive supply chain spent decades resolving questions like these through binding contracts and established liability frameworks. This MoU attempts to assemble those relationships from scratch, between four companies that have never jointly deployed autonomous vehicles, for a technology deployment none of them has individually demonstrated at the targeted scale.

When nobody knows which role captures value, assembling a consortium of specialists is as defensible a bet as any other. Even when no one in the consortium has done this before.

## Two Companies, Opposite Answers

If the rest of the industry is experimenting with where value lives, two companies have actually delivered autonomous services at commercial scale. And they have reached opposite conclusions.

Even Waymo, the company with the strongest position in the industry, is still experimenting. Its own app serves over 500,000 trips per week. But internationally, it is going direct in London with its own brand and operational infrastructure, while in Tokyo it partnered with the country's largest taxi company and leading ride-hailing app. The company with the most operational data in autonomous mobility has not settled on a single distribution strategy.

Apollo Go is doing the opposite. In China, it runs its own consumer app (all rides fully driverless since February 2025). But internationally, it is pursuing every distribution model at once.

Uber integration in Dubai. An institutional partnership with PostAuto in Switzerland deploying 25 L4 vehicles for on-demand public transit, where PostAuto owns the vehicles, runs the operations, and holds the data under Swiss law. Apollo Go provides the technology. Where Waymo consolidates, Apollo Go adapts to whatever distribution channel each market offers.

Two companies with the most operational data in autonomous mobility have reached opposing conclusions about where value sits. The answer is not settled. It may not be settleable for years.

Verne, watching both models from Zagreb, chose a third path: operate someone else's technology on someone else's platform, in vehicles someone else built. A pure operator play. Whether that position captures meaningful value or gets squeezed between the technology provider above and the platform below is exactly the question this entire industry is trying to answer.

## The Work Nobody Planned For

Every deal announced in this space generates a press release. Vehicle supplier confirmed. Technology partner selected. Platform integration agreed. The visible roles get named.

What rarely gets named is the operational layer underneath: who runs the depot, who charges the vehicles overnight, who handles maintenance when something breaks, who dispatches the field technician when a car stops responding.

Transdev, one of the world's largest private public transport operators (active in 19 countries), has been observed operating Waymo's Dallas depot behind Avis branding. Transdev also built the full operational structure for the Babcock Ranch autonomous vehicle shuttle: software, service center, fleet management. A company that rarely appears in AV industry coverage may be quietly building one of its most durable positions.

Avis is converting rental car lots into AV fleet depots. In Nashville, Lyft's Flexdrive subsidiary manages the physical infrastructure for Waymo's fleet: charging, cleaning, maintenance. An emerging division of labor: Avis owns the real estate and vehicles, Transdev manages operations and calibration, Lyft manages dispatch and customer logistics.

This is a layer of the value chain that no one designed. It is assembling itself from adjacent industries because the technology companies that built autonomous vehicles did not build the operational infrastructure those vehicles require. These are not glamorous roles, and they are harder than they look. They do not attract billion-dollar venture rounds. And they may turn out to be among the most durable positions in the entire value chain, precisely because they are hard to replicate and easy to undervalue.

Verne operates its Zagreb fleet with safety drivers in the vehicles. Someone trains those drivers. Someone manages the shift schedule. Someone handles the vehicle when it returns to base. That operational work does not appear in the press release. It appears in the P&L.

## The Searching Is the Mechanism

Verne tried building vehicles, partnering with one technology provider, switching to another, and operating on a platform. Hyundai is supplying every stack that will take its hardware while quietly building its own. NVIDIA is investing in, competing with, and attempting to platform the entire sector simultaneously. Four companies signed a non-binding memorandum to build something none of them has proven alone. The two companies that have actually scaled the technology reached opposite conclusions about where value lives. And underneath all of it, companies from public transport, car rental, and ride-hail are building an operational layer that nobody designed.

This is not dysfunction. This is what an industry looks like when the technology has arrived but the business model has not.

The traditional automotive value chain was not designed either. It accreted over decades of competitive pressure, regulatory evolution, and market learning. What we are watching now is a compressed version of that process: an entire industry trying on roles, signing non-binding agreements, hedging across competing architectures, and watching to see which positions survive contact with paying customers and real-world operations.

Verne is still searching. Started as a builder. Became a technology partner's client. Ended up a fleet operator on someone else's platform, running someone else's cars, with permits pending in a dozen markets. Three positions in two years. The next twelve months may add another.

Not all of these positions will survive. A pure operator that owns neither the technology nor the customer relationship is exposed on both sides. But in an industry where the value chain is still being assembled, the companies that keep moving are at least learning which roles hold weight and which ones don't. The ones standing still are just hoping they guessed right.
