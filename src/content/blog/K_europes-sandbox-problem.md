PostAuto has been running autonomous vehicles on Swiss public roads since 2016. First public transport operator in the world to do it. Google visited. Toyota visited.

Their first partner, Navya, went bankrupt in 2023.

When PostAuto was ready to move from pilots to a real on-demand service, they surveyed the global market for a Level 4 technology partner. [Martin Neubauer](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=otLgk7vAs9I), PostAuto's Head of Autonomous Mobility, described the search plainly: after surveying the global market, there were not many capable partners looking into Europe.

Their choice: Baidu Apollo. Twenty-five L4 vehicles for on-demand service in eastern Switzerland. PostAuto owns the vehicles, runs the operations, holds the data under Swiss law. The operational expertise is entirely European. The technology is Chinese.

This is not a story about one company making one decision. It is the European AV industry in miniature.

---

## 400 Pilots, Zero Services

At an EU Parliament event on autonomous vehicles in 2026, the assessment was blunt. More than 400 pilot projects across Europe. Not one has produced a commercial service. The US has at least four paid autonomous ride services operating today. [Waymo alone serves over 500,000 trips per week](https://waymo.com/blog/shorts/waymo-recently-hosted-an-event-in-tokyo-to-share-more-about-our-autonomous/). Baidu's Apollo Go in China handles hundreds of thousands more.

Europe's contribution: pilots. Well-funded, well-documented, frequently cited at conferences. None carrying paying passengers at commercial scale.

Pierfrancesco Maran put it directly: "Now or we scale up or it's too late." The ambition is shared. The regulatory path from pilot to commercial service exists in theory. No one has completed it yet.

Spain alone has regulatory sandboxes in five cities. Switzerland, which is not an EU member but aligns closely with European vehicle standards, enacted an Ordinance on Automated Driving that explicitly permits fully driverless Level 4 vehicles on authorized roads. Even there, every vehicle still operates under the separate pilot framework. Across the EU, no AV has received type approval yet.

The legal containers exist. They are empty.

## The Companies That Showed Up

The global AV industry has moved from proving concepts to shipping products. That transition is visible in the US, where Waymo's 6th-generation platform targets tens of thousands of vehicles per year. In China, where Baidu's Apollo Go has completed tens of millions of rides. In the Middle East, where WeRide runs fully driverless operations with Uber in Abu Dhabi.

It is visible in Europe too. But the companies driving it are not European.

WeRide is active in four European markets. Momenta has set up a European headquarters in Stuttgart. [Uber, Verne, and Pony.ai announced what they call "Europe's first commercial robotaxi service"](https://investor.uber.com/news-events/news/press-release-details/2026/Verne-Pony-ai-and-Uber-Partner-to-Launch-Europes-First-Commercial-Robotaxi-Service/default.aspx) in Croatia. Baidu is expanding into Switzerland, the UK, and Germany. Several of these are partnering with Uber. All of them are backed by OEM relationships with European and global manufacturers.

These companies still face Europe's regulatory path. Testing permits come first. Commercial operations require type approval that does not yet exist in practice. But they are establishing presence and positioning for when the commercial pathway opens.

PostAuto chose Baidu for a specific reason. They went to Wuhan. They watched the fleet operate at scale. They saw a technology that was not just proven but accepted by passengers as routine. Neubauer's observation: in Wuhan, autonomous vehicles on public roads had become routine. Nothing special anymore. That operational maturity is what PostAuto was buying. Not a pilot. A product.

No European company offered that.

## Three Paths, Three Bottlenecks

Europe has the frameworks. The EU's type-approval architecture, with national layers on top in Germany and France. Switzerland's own ordinance. The UK's Automated Vehicles Act. A UNECE regulation aiming to harmonize AV type approval globally is expected to enter force in 2027, with an EU implementing regulation following in 2028. Arguably the most structured regulatory path in the world. But structure without commercial deployment is just process. The path from testing permit to paid service is long, expensive, and largely unproven.

The technology gap is harder to dismiss. Navya is bankrupt. EasyMile has retreated from public roads to airports and industrial sites. No European company operates a paid autonomous ride service.

There is real European activity. Holon is developing purpose-built autonomous shuttles. Wayve has secured OEM partnerships across multiple manufacturers. Others are in active development. But even the furthest along are pre-commercial, working through the milestones that separate testing from a live service.

What makes Europe's situation genuinely complex is that every plausible path to commercial autonomous services here faces a different bottleneck.

Chinese companies have proven their technology at scale. But none of them have type-approved vehicles for the European market. Every deployment so far operates under testing permits. Going from a pilot framework to full type approval is not an incremental step. It is a separate regulatory process that no autonomous vehicle has completed in the EU yet.

US companies, Waymo above all, are further along than anyone. But entering Europe under testing permits holds little appeal when you are already running commercial services elsewhere. Investors expect revenue, not more pilots. Their next-generation vehicle is built by Zeekr, a Geely subsidiary, and production is already underway. But those vehicles still need EU type approval before they can operate commercially in the EU.

There is also a business model question. Outside London, where Waymo is already in progress, most European cities will expect autonomous services to integrate with public transport rather than compete with it as standalone ridehailing. That is a fundamentally different service model than what works in Phoenix or San Francisco.

European operators understand that model. Public transport operators, fleet managers, and shared mobility platforms are the natural candidates to run autonomous services. They understand city integration, passenger operations, and fleet logistics in ways that outside technology providers do not. What they lack is the autonomous driving technology itself.

Three paths. Three different gaps. None of them close quickly, and it is not obvious which converges first.

PostAuto tried building that bridge for seven years. They ended up crossing it with a partner from Wuhan.

---

## The Technology Gap Was Not a Choice

The framing of a race is tempting but misleading. Current global robotaxi deployments amount to a few thousand vehicles. In the grand scheme of transportation, that is a pittance. Autonomous mobility will not be winner-take-all. Markets are local, needs vary by city and region, and the timeline for this technology to become routine is measured in decades, not quarters. There will be room for multiple models, multiple providers, and multiple approaches.

Europe's regulatory path reflects that reality. Cross-border type approval, safety validation, interoperability across 27 member states. If the first European commercial service launches on vehicles that are fully type-approved and can operate across borders, that is not a consolation prize. It is a structural advantage that neither the US nor China currently offers.

But the regulatory path was a choice. The technology gap was not. No European policymaker decided to let Navya go bankrupt or to underinvest in L4 development. That happened through fragmented funding, smaller venture markets, and a startup ecosystem that could not sustain the capital intensity of autonomous driving. The regulatory frameworks may prove durable. The absence of competitive European technology is a problem that gets harder to reverse the longer it persists.

PostAuto's profile is not unique. Across Europe, public transport operators with years of AV pilot experience, deep regulatory relationships, and full ownership of their fleet and data are the natural foundation for autonomous services. And when one of the earliest among them was ready to move, the partner they needed was in Wuhan.

The question is whether Europe builds what comes after the sandbox, or whether the companies that already built the product will build it for them.
