# Europe’s Next Step for Commercial Robotaxis

The first driverless fare is not simply another stage of testing. It is the start of a different kind of service.

For the passenger, the ride may look almost identical. For the operator, city, and regulator, the permission is of a different kind. The vehicle must now take responsibility for the full driving task without a human fallback inside it. The operating system around it must be able to support it when the road presents something it cannot resolve. And the company is no longer only learning at its own expense. It is charging residents for transport in public space.

When should it be allowed to do that?

A vehicle might operate safely in a narrow test environment, or even carry paying passengers with a safety operator onboard, without being ready to run a driverless service. It might manage a defined set of streets, a limited fleet, daylight hours, favourable weather, and an operating team ready to help when it encounters something it cannot resolve. That is meaningful progress. It does not yet show that the service can take responsibility without a safety driver.

European countries have learned how to authorise automated-vehicle testing. The harder next question is how a programme moves from controlled testing or supervised paid service into a driverless service that can continue earning the right to grow.

## The first driverless fare requires a different permission

Testing, supervised commercial service, and driverless commercial service do different jobs.

Testing is meant to generate evidence. It is expected to be limited, supervised, and costly for the company conducting it. The operator bears the expense of learning where the system works, where it does not, and what needs to improve before the next stage. A supervised paid service can be a useful bridge: it tests demand and service operations while retaining a person inside the vehicle who can intervene.

But a driverless paid service changes the relationship again. The person inside the vehicle is no longer the operational fallback. Passengers are no longer merely participating in a technical programme. They are paying for a transport service that is expected to get them to their destination safely and reliably. A city is no longer hosting an experiment with a defined horizon. It is allowing a private operator to take fares from residents and occupy public space as part of everyday mobility.

That does not mean a driverless commercial service should wait until the vehicle has proved universal readiness. A supervised paid service may generate revenue, but it retains the cost and operating model of a person inside the vehicle. If the only route to a driverless fare is to demonstrate broad readiness before that person can be removed, commercialisation becomes an expensive endurance contest that favours companies able to fund years of operation before the market has learned whether their service can work without that fallback.

But the opposite mistake is just as real. Once a company can make money without the cost of a safety driver, it may have less reason to move beyond a service that is merely good enough. Revenue can finance maturity. It can also make a constrained, weakly improving operation commercially comfortable. The public should not be asked to finance an indefinite test programme under a different label.

This is why the first right to charge without a safety driver should not be treated as a finish line. It should be a conditional permission: narrow enough to reflect what the company has actually demonstrated, serious enough to recognise that passengers are receiving a real service, and revisitable as the operation learns or fails to learn.

## A regulatory state before broad driverless deployment

Recently proposed [amendments to the EU Automotive Omnibus](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/doceo/document/CJ76-AM-789933_EN.pdf) would create a time-limited type-approval route for small-series, fully automated vehicles in commercial pre-deployment. The proposed three-year term, in-use monitoring, and renewal make the missing question visible: what should a company have to show before its approval is renewed or its scope is expanded? Type approval would not, by itself, settle the separate question of permission to run a commercial transport service.

The programmes now emerging across Europe make the gap visible. Zagreb offers the closest live transition: [Verne began a commercial service with an onboard operator](https://investor.uber.com/news-events/news/press-release-details/2026/Uber-Launches-Autonomous-Rides-in-Europe/default.aspx), then [announced fully driverless passenger test rides](https://ir.pony.ai/news-releases/news-release-details/pony-ai-inc-and-verne-kick-fully-driverless-robotaxi-test-rides) for invited users on a 22-kilometre public-road route. [Luxembourg's Pony.ai, Bolt and Stellantis programme](https://ir.pony.ai/news-releases/news-release-details/pony-ai-inc-announces-autonomous-mobility-testing-program) is explicitly a pilot to validate safety, performance and regulatory readiness. In Switzerland, the [framework permits driverless vehicles on defined, locally authorised routes under remote supervision](https://www.astra.admin.ch/de/verordnung-ueber-das-automatisierte-fahren), while the [Furttal robotaxi project remains in its test phase](https://blog.astra.admin.ch/die-schweiz-pionierin-der-intelligenten-mobilitaet/). These are not signs that nothing is happening. They show that Europe can authorise important parts of the journey.

But none of them, on their own, answers the commercial question. A company may be allowed to test, carry selected passengers, or charge fares within a defined service. That does not automatically establish how it earns the right to add the next neighbourhood, operating condition, vehicle, or country. A programme can be repeatable while still not being scalable if every expansion starts as another bespoke regulatory negotiation.

The EU still sits between national testing or limited-deployment routes and a broad driverless commercial threshold that has yet to become a demonstrated path to service at scale. An intermediate commercial permission would acknowledge that a limited driverless service can be useful before a company is ready to operate everywhere. It would also acknowledge that a company serving paying passengers without a safety driver has obligations that do not disappear because it is still learning.

That distinction matters for competition as well as safety. The same amendments would make defined authorisations more portable across the EU. Evidence collected in one EU market should not become worthless at the next border.

But portability cannot mean pretending that all local conditions are interchangeable. Roads, emergency arrangements, weather, passenger needs, and local transport systems vary. The goal should be a portable core: an operator’s demonstrated ability to run a safe, reliable, improving service, combined with justified adjustments to the ODD in each place it serves.

## Permission should have a memory

What should determine whether a company keeps that commercial permission, or earns the right to expand it?

Not a single universal KPI. Imagine that a vehicle struggles with a particular configuration of roadworks: it stops too often, asks for help, or cannot resolve the situation cleanly. The important question is not whether the event happened once. Six months later, on comparable streets and at comparable scale, what has changed?

A growing fleet will encounter new situations, especially as it operates in a broader ODD. More trips will surface more unusual combinations of people, vehicles, roadworks, weather, events, and system failures. Finding a new failure mode is not automatically a sign that a programme is unsafe. It may be evidence that the company is seeing the real world clearly enough to learn from it.

What matters is what happens to known problems.

If a particular kind of incident has been identified, does it recur less often in comparable conditions? If it does recur, is it less severe? Is it resolved more reliably? Does the burden of unresolved, safety-relevant events fall relative to the relevant scale of service: comparable trips, operating hours, fleet size, or exposure in the same ODD?

This is a rate-of-learning test. It does not ask a company to promise that unusual events will disappear. It asks whether the service becomes more capable because it has encountered them.

That makes the commercial permission more than a one-time regulatory decision. It has to be renewed in practice. The company can earn revenue and build a business. In return, it must show that its operation is becoming safer and more dependable as it takes on more public responsibility. A regulator can expand, hold, constrain, or withdraw the permission based on that trajectory, rather than treating accumulated miles or a completed document set as the whole story.

## Remote assistance is where the operating model is tested

Once the safety driver is removed, remote support becomes part of the service's safety case. [EU type-approval rules](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:02022R1426-20260324) already draw one boundary: remote support may assist, but the ADS remains responsible for driving.

Commercial deployment raises a further question: what must the operating system around the vehicle be able to do when the ADS needs support? California’s [SB 1246](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billTextClient.xhtml?bill_id=202520260SB1246) offers a useful comparison. It begins to specify the operational questions a commercial driverless service must answer: how quickly a human can be reached, whether the operator can handle simultaneous incidents, how an obstruction is cleared, and what records show that these promises hold in practice.

The EU does not need to copy California’s rules or prescribe a universal operator-to-vehicle ratio. But the example shows how operational accountability can become more visible as a service matures. A credible company should be able to choose how it builds its support model. A city and regulator should be able to see whether that model works at the scope of service being sold.

Those expectations should become more precise as the industry learns from real operation. The task is to make them proportionate and portable: rigorous enough to protect passengers, first responders, and other road users, without turning every expansion into a new operational bottleneck.

## A route to service, not an easier way around responsibility

The EU needs a way to sequence the evidence required for a driverless service. A limited commercial pathway would let an operator demonstrate maturity in real operation as it adds vehicles, routes, operating conditions, and passengers.

That pathway fails if every city invents its own opaque requirements and only the largest companies can navigate the resulting patchwork. It works when common evidence expectations can travel across markets, while local conditions remain local by design.

A supervised paid robotaxi should not be treated as proof that it is ready to operate without a safety driver. A company should be able to earn that next permission through a limited driverless service, while remaining accountable for what it has not yet proved.

The empty front seat is not proof that the vehicle is finished. It is the point at which regulators decide whether revenue buys a path to better evidence, or simply makes an unfinished operating model comfortable. A credible operator should be able to earn the right to remove that fallback in a limited service. It should then have to keep earning the right to do more.
