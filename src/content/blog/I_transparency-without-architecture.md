When a number like "70 remote assistance agents" breaks into a Senate hearing, two conversations happen at once.

The one you can see: journalists, commentators, and advocates debate what it means. Is offshoring safety-critical jobs acceptable? Is 70 agents for 3,000 vehicles a lot or a little? Are driverless cars as autonomous as advertised?

The one you can't: people who work with these systems know the metric matters — and they also know that "70" without context tells you almost nothing.

Both conversations are real. Neither is complete. And the gap between them reveals something the ratio debate itself obscures.

---

## What the Letter Actually Is

The 70-agent figure didn't come from the Senate hearing itself. It came from a letter Waymo sent to Senator Markey on February 17, 2026, responding to questions his office had raised after testimony about remote operations.

What the letter actually is matters more than the number it contains.

Ten pages. Signed by Ryan McNamara, VP and Global Head of Operations. Covering: remote assistance architecture, operator qualifications, background screening requirements for US and Philippines-based agents, shift structures, fatigue protocols, cybersecurity practices including hardware-backed multi-factor authentication and floor-level device restrictions, latency data broken down by facility (roughly 150ms median one-way for US centers, roughly 250ms for Philippines), and confirmation of a 2025 independent TÜV SÜD audit that specifically examined remote assistance practices against the AVSC consortium's industry best practice standard.

Read it and you have a coherent, navigable picture of how Waymo's remote operations actually work.

That's the thing worth sitting with. Not the agent count. The document itself.

---

## What Waymo Gets Right

Waymo's public safety materials are genuinely well-organized. Downloadable crash records. Peer-reviewed methodology. Research partnerships with IIHS and Swiss Re. Detailed collision data covering 127 million fully autonomous miles (as of February 17).

What's been absent is the connective tissue. The document that places remote operations inside the broader safety case. That explains what the US-based Event Response Team handles versus the Philippines-based agents, and why the distinction is operationally meaningful. That shows how fleet operations decisions connect to safety outcomes. Until the Senator asked, that synthesis didn't exist anywhere in public-facing form.

After the Senator asked, it appeared. In ten days.

That's not a culture of secrecy. It's not negligence either. It's a structural pattern: accountability assembles under external pressure but isn't designed to exist permanently. The capability to produce the document was always there. The mechanism that would make it permanent, and public, without political forcing wasn't.

---

## Why the CBI Logic Holds (and Why That's the Problem)

On some of the specific questions Senator Markey raised, Waymo declined to answer. Operator-to-vehicle ratios. Granular incident logs. Specific system boundaries. Their reasoning: this constitutes Confidential Business Information that, if disclosed, would give competitors deep insight into operational scaling strategies.

I understand why they made that call.

If Waymo publishes a concurrent agent ratio, a competitor can announce a similar number built on a different definition of "concurrent," a different fleet composition, a different scope for what counts as an agent. The public has no way to evaluate whether the numbers mean the same thing. Disclosure creates a floor others can game without any shared methodology that would make the comparison meaningful.

That's a rational business decision. The point here is narrower: even accepting the CBI logic on those specific items, the February 17 letter shows that a comprehensive, coherent accountability document was fully producible without crossing any of those lines. It exists. It just required a Senator.

---

## Who Bears the Cost

When accountability mechanisms break down, companies pay eventually. Cruise is the clearest example: after covering up the full circumstances of the October 2023 pedestrian incident, the company effectively shut down and GM lost billions.

But the timing matters.

The public bears the cost first. Cities negotiating operating permits are doing it now, evaluating safety from fragments, with no shared language for asking the right questions. Regulators setting safety standards are working from a floor that captures crash data after thresholds are crossed but nothing predictive before them. Riders deciding whether to get in the car are making that decision without the connective tissue that the February 17 letter contains.

None of them currently can access what the Senator received in ten days.

---

## The Reactive vs. Structural Distinction

This is the crux.

Regulatory disclosure exists. NHTSA's Standing General Order requires crash reports above set thresholds. State regulators in California have additional requirements. Waymo does not redact collision narratives submitted to NHTSA, which is a meaningful commitment. The February 17 letter itself was a responsive disclosure to a formal congressional inquiry.

What doesn't exist is the permanent public-facing layer. The synthesis that would let a city official, a regulator, or a curious member of the public understand how remote operations fit into the safety case without needing to write a letter to a Senator first.

The distinction isn't between transparent and opaque. It's between reactive and structural. Reactive transparency exists: it surfaces under external pressure, through regulatory processes, through congressional inquiries, in response to incidents. It produces documents like the February 17 letter. That document is good. The problem is its trigger.

Structural transparency would mean the document exists without the trigger. Updated regularly. Accessible without a congressional inquiry as the forcing function.

The companies that have solved this are not the ones that shared the most. They're the ones that built the architecture so the question doesn't have to be forced.

---

## What the Letter Proves Is Possible

The February 17 letter is useful precisely because it shows what a coherent accountability document for remote operations looks like when someone requires it to exist.

Latency by facility. Staffing split between US and Philippines. The specific roles the Event Response Team handles (collisions, law enforcement interface, regulatory reporting, towing coordination) versus what Philippines-based agents handle. Training and retraining frequency. Random drug testing rates. Cybersecurity protocols. Third-party audit confirmation from TÜV SÜD.

None of this required disclosing the CBI items Waymo withheld. The coherent picture was fully producible within those limits. Which means the absence of a permanent version of this document isn't a disclosure problem. It's an architecture problem. Nobody owns the ongoing synthesis. Nobody has been asked to.

That may be less a deliberate choice than a gap nobody has explicitly owned. The focus has been on producing data and defending what doesn't need disclosing. The always-on public layer, the one that doesn't require a Senator, is the piece that falls between the teams: safety research, operations, legal, engineering. Each coherent in isolation. Not connected for an external audience.

The 70-agent debate is not really about 70 agents.

It's about whether the capability to produce accountability, which clearly exists, is only ever triggered by external pressure, or whether it can be built to exist on its own terms.

Right now it requires a Senator. That's a structural problem, not a transparency one. And it's the industry's to solve.
