Everyone's talking about the AI behind robotaxis. The neural networks, the training data, the compute. Almost nobody's talking about the humans.

Here's the reality: "driverless" cars aren't driverless yet.

Behind every autonomous fleet is a workforce you never see. Remote assistance operators resolving edge cases. Rider support teams handling passenger issues. Hub crews managing vehicle maintenance, cleaning, and charging. Development teams pushing software updates. And that's before you count production jobs building the vehicles.

The industry talks about autonomy like it's a software problem with a software solution. But the operational reality is far messier.

## What Remote Operations Actually Looks Like

When most people imagine remote operators for robotaxis, they picture someone with a steering wheel, ready to take over driving at any moment. But is it really like that?

The reality is different. Most remote interventions don't involve driving at all. They involve:

- **Giving the vehicle permission to proceed** when it encounters an ambiguous situation
- **Helping interpret edge cases** the AI wasn't trained to handle
- **Assisting confused passengers** who don't understand the system
- **Monitoring fleet health** and dispatching maintenance

There are typically two distinct roles. Remote assistance handles technical challenges that require understanding the vehicle's perception and decision-making. Rider support handles customer experience. Both require different skills, but neither involves much actual driving.

Teleoperation (remote driving) exists, but it's the exception. It's slow, bandwidth-intensive, and comes with latency that makes it impractical for most situations. The real work is decision support, not driving.

## The Ratio Problem

Here's where it gets interesting.

The economics of autonomous fleets depend on the ratio of remote operators to vehicles. A 1:1 ratio defeats the purpose. You've just replaced an in-vehicle driver with a remote one. The goal is 1:many.

But most AV companies today operate somewhere between 1:1 and 1:10. Getting to 1:50 or 1:100 is the holy grail. And it's proving harder than expected.

Why? Because the operators need an unusual combination of skills:

- **Calm under pressure** for emergency situations
- **Empathy** for distressed passengers
- **Technical literacy** to understand a deeply complex system
- **Judgment** to make split-second decisions with incomplete information

That profile is rare. And training for it is hard because the job has never existed at scale before. There's no talent pipeline. No established curriculum. No industry certification.

## What Germany Identified

Germany's autonomous vehicle regulation (AFGBV §14) is one of the few frameworks that takes operator qualifications seriously.

To serve as a "Technische Aufsicht" (technical supervisor) for autonomous vehicles in Germany, you need:

- An **engineering degree** (mechanical, electrical, vehicle, or aerospace engineering) or state-certified technician qualification
- **Manufacturer-specific training** on the autonomous vehicle
- **Annual recertification** including practical exams with simulated operational failures
- A **valid driver's license** for the vehicle class
- A **clean driving record** (maximum 3 points)

Whether engineering degrees are strictly necessary remains to be seen. Perhaps a well-designed technician certification would suffice. The industry is too early to know what the right bar is.

But Germany got something important right: this is not a call center job. These roles require formal qualifications, ongoing training, and demonstrated competency. That recognition alone puts them ahead of most regulatory frameworks.

The implication? Higher costs, a smaller talent pool, and longer ramp-up times. But also a more honest view of what safe operations require.

## When Ratios Collapse

Even with well-trained operators and optimized ratios, there's a failure mode nobody talks about: fleet-wide incidents.

When Waymo's vehicles got stuck during the San Francisco blackout, every car in the affected area needed attention simultaneously. Your carefully planned 1:many ratio doesn't matter when "many" all need help at once.

This is the problem with optimizing for average case. Remote operations need to handle peak load, not just typical load. And peak load in autonomy can mean every vehicle in your fleet hitting an edge case at the same moment.

How do you staff for that? How do you train for scenarios you've never seen? These are open questions the industry hasn't answered.

## The Real Scaling Problem

The autonomous vehicle industry has poured billions into making the AI smarter. Bigger models, more training data, better sensors. And that work matters.

But there's a parallel problem that's gotten far less attention: building the human infrastructure to operate these fleets at scale.

That means:
- Defining job roles that don't exist yet
- Creating training programs for skills that have never been formalized
- Recruiting from talent pools that may not be obvious (who makes a good remote operator?)
- Building organizational structures that can scale from dozens to thousands of operators
- Developing tools that let operators work effectively at higher ratios

The "driverless" future depends on building a workforce that doesn't exist yet, for a job that's never been done at scale, with a skill profile that's hard to find and harder to train.

That might be the hardest scaling problem in autonomy. And almost no one is talking about it.
