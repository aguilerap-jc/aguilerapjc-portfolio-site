When we discuss autonomous vehicles, the conversation centers on perception systems and neural networks. Waymo's rider experience. Tesla's FSD progress. Billion-dollar funding rounds and robotaxi launch dates.

Fleet management software barely gets a mention.

Which is strange, because without it, none of this works at scale.

## A Day in the Life of an Autonomous Fleet

Imagine you're operating 500 autonomous vehicles in a major city.

Before a single ride happens, each vehicle needs verification. The requirements vary by jurisdiction. Daily or pre-departure inspections. Incident reporting within days. Ongoing compliance documentation. Communication links between vehicles and remote operators. In Germany, that means ISO 9001 standards. In California, quarterly CPUC reports. In the UK, civil sanctions for non-compliance.

Now layer in the operational reality. Some vehicles need maintenance. Others need charging. Software updates need deployment. One vehicle reported a sensor anomaly overnight. Three are due for comprehensive inspection.

How do you orchestrate this?

Traditional fleets have similar inspection mandates. But they rely on drivers to perform them. A trucker walks around, kicks the tires, checks the mirrors, signs off. Autonomous fleets need something different - whether that's technicians at hubs, automated diagnostics, or some combination. The operational model is still being figured out.

Traditional fleet management platforms weren't designed for this. They assume a human in the loop. They weren't built for vehicles that need sensor verification at scale, regulatory compliance tracking across multiple jurisdictions, or integration with remote assistance systems.

And when things go wrong, they go wrong at scale.

In 2023, videos circulated of Cruise and Waymo vehicles blocking streets in San Francisco. Multiple robotaxis frozen in the same location, unable to proceed. These incidents weren't just perception failures. They revealed a fleet coordination gap: how do you detect when multiple vehicles are hitting the same problem? How do you diagnose whether it's a map issue, a software bug, or a real-world obstacle? And how do you resolve it at fleet scale, not vehicle by vehicle?

Traditional fleet software tracks vehicles individually. It doesn't ask "why are 12 of my vehicles all stopped within 200 meters of each other?"

The software layer that makes autonomous fleet operations possible is genuinely different from what exists today.

## Where Is It?

The major AV players are building operational infrastructure. But they're building it for themselves.

Waymo operates hundreds of thousands of weekly trips across multiple cities. That requires sophisticated tooling for dispatch, maintenance, remote assistance, and fleet optimization. Tesla is preparing robotaxi services. The large ride-hailing platforms are integrating AV partners into their networks.

None of this is available externally.

When you look for third-party solutions, the landscape is thin. A handful of startups offer fleet management platforms with AV-specific features. Some vehicle manufacturers are bundling operational software with hardware. A few logistics-focused players are extending into autonomous operations.

But for a global industry expecting to deploy millions of autonomous vehicles across diverse regulatory environments, the options remain limited.

Why?

Building this is hard. You need deep integration with vehicle systems, regulatory expertise across jurisdictions, and the ability to handle edge cases traditional fleet software never anticipated: sensor degradation, remote takeover protocols, compliance documentation.

There's also a market access problem. Traditional fleet management could work with aftermarket solutions - plug in a dongle, pull OBD data, integrate loosely. Autonomous vehicles don't work that way. The data lives deep in proprietary systems. Getting access requires OEM partnerships. But OEMs are either building their own fleet software or partnering exclusively with well-resourced players.

For a startup, the path is narrow: existing fleet expertise, strong industry relationships, and probably a regulatory angle. Even then, regulations are immature and shifting. Flexibility isn't optional.

Until recently, the market was also too small to justify the investment. The few companies operating AVs at scale built custom solutions. The long tail of future operators wasn't buying yet.

That may be starting to change.

## The Hidden Divide

Consider who wants to operate autonomous vehicles.

There's Waymo, Uber, Tesla. Companies with thousands of engineers and billions in capital. They can build whatever they need.

Then there's everyone else. Public transport operators. Regional mobility providers. Logistics companies. Airport authorities. Municipalities exploring autonomous shuttles. These organizations want to deploy AVs but can't build fleet management software from scratch. They need to buy it. And right now, options are scarce.

The result: a widening gap. Well-resourced early adopters move forward. The long tail of operators waits for infrastructure that's still emerging.

## An Overlooked Opportunity

The AV industry is obsessed with the driving problem. Understandably. It's genuinely hard.

But the operational problem is also hard. And it's only starting to be addressed.

The platform that makes autonomous fleet operations accessible, not just for tech giants but for any mobility operator, will control a significant piece of the ecosystem.

Multi-OEM vehicle support. Regulatory compliance tracking across jurisdictions. Sensor health monitoring. Maintenance optimization. Charging coordination. Remote assistance integration. Software deployment management.

It's infrastructure work. It's not glamorous. It won't generate headlines about robotaxi launches or breakthrough AI capabilities.

But it might determine who actually gets to participate in autonomous mobility, and who gets left behind.

The industry talks about democratizing mobility. But democratization requires more than technology. It requires infrastructure that any operator can access - not just companies with billions in capital. Without it, autonomous vehicles remain a solution for wealthy cities and well-funded tech companies. Everyone else waits.

The opportunity is still wide open. The question is who builds it.
